an industrial food factory disguised as a garden, and the largest single capital work on the estate after the house, an enclosure of 0.4 to 2.0 hectares
whose walls are not a boundary but the growing apparatus - the south-facing inner face of the north wall is the most valuable 300 square meters on the estate
holding peaches
nectarines and figs that will not ripen in the open in Britain, and the entire enclosure exists to create that face and to shelter the rest; walls built of brick even where the whole estate is stone, because brick holds and releases heat and stone does not, which is why a stone house frequently has a brick kitchen garden and that mismatch is the giveaway; in the most ambitious examples a hot wall
with flues built into the thickness of the south-facing wall and stokeholes at its base, so the wall itself is heated through the spring to bring the blossom on and keep the frost off, which is the same technique as the orangery at record 18 applied to a wall 80 m long; a plan of four quarters divided by two gravel walks crossing at a dipping tank
which took one man six weeks a year to tie in; a frame ground with forty lights on the outside of the garden or in a slip, which is where the real production happens; forcing pits and hot beds made with 40 tonnes of stable dung a year from record 46, which is the engine of the whole operation; a mushroom house
a tool store and the back-of-garden range at records 51 and 53; and a crop plan that supplied a household of fifty with every vegetable and every fruit it ate
365 days a year, including pineapples
grapes
peaches and melons out of season, which is the achievement and is routinely underestimated
