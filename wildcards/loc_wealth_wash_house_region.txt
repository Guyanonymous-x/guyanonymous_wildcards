the English laundry as the type, with the four-times-a-year great wash giving way to the weekly wash around 1830-50 as cotton replaces linen and soap gets cheaper

the Irish version
the French buanderie, with a lavoir or a river-bank washing place doing much of the work in smaller households and a professional blanchisseuse in the towns
the Italian and Spanish versions, which wash at a communal stone trough or a river and dry on walls and roofs, so the whole operation is outdoors and public
the Dutch version, tiled and formidable
the Scandinavian version, with a separate wash house and a boiling copper shared between farms
the American plantation laundry, a separate building worked by enslaved women, and the Gilded Age laundry, which is mechanized earlier and more thoroughly than any English equivalent; and the Indian colonial household, where the washing goes out to a dhobi at a ghat, which removes the room from the house entirely
