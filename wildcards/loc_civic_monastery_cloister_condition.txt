the cloister of 1380 at its fullest, vaulted, traceried, carreled and in use for sixteen hours a day by a community of forty, which is the arrangement described here
the cloister of 1450 with the north walk glazed as an experiment and the carrels improved, which a few houses did
the cloister of 1540 at the Dissolution, the lead stripped from the walk roofs within months because lead was the point, the vaults then failing within two winters
the cloister of 1600 as a quarry, with the tracery robbed for a farmhouse and the garth grazed
the cloister of 1850 as a cathedral close's cloister, repaired, re-glazed, floored and used as a route between the deanery and the church, which is the condition of nearly every complete survivor in England and is the reason they feel like corridors rather than workplaces
the cloister of 1880 as a school's covered way
the cloister of 2026 as a visitor route with a shop at one corner, a coffee cart in the garth on event days, forty-four bays of tracery conserved at considerable expense and a laminated plan on a stand
the cloister as a consolidated ruin, with the walk walls and the bench at full height, the vaults gone, the garth mown and the ledger slabs under turf, which is the commonest surviving condition in Britain and is managed as a monument rather than a building; and the cloister open to the weather and unmanaged, where the stone survives almost indefinitely and the garth becomes scrub and sycamore in twenty years
