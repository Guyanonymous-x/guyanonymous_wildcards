the single largest flow of material into the building and the second largest out of it, and the reason the service court is laid out as it is, a house of this size burning 200-450 tonnes of coal and coke a year, which is four to nine tonnes a week in winter
every kilogram of which arrives by cart or by rail and is then carried by hand in scuttles up to between thirty and sixty grates, and whose residue goes out as thirty to seventy tonnes of ash and clinker a year; brick-vaulted bays beneath the yard or in the basement, each of 10-15 tonnes capacity
with timber stop-boards in iron uprights so the face can be worked down and new coal tipped on top without mixing the grades; cast-iron coal plates set into the yard paving above each bay
with counterweighted lids, so the cart tips straight down a chute and no coal is ever carried into the building at ground level, which is the whole purpose of the arrangement and is why a coal plate is the most reliable surviving evidence of where a service court was; a hard yard of blue engineering brick or granite sett laid to a fall with a gully, because the yard is swept and hosed; an ash pit of brick with an iron cover
sunk so that ash can be tipped rather than lifted; a cinder sifter
since cinders were recovered and reburnt and that recovery was somebody's accountable duty; a walled dust bin for the sweepings; a soot barrel, because soot was a saleable commodity; a stand pipe; and a yard entrance from the back drive wide enough for a cart, which is the gate the house's whole fuel supply comes through
