at the tangihanga the dead woman in her seventies lies in an open coffin on the marae before the carved meeting house, women kin in their forties to eighties in black dresses with wreaths of green kawakawa leaves on their heads seated beside her
in the old mourning, kin in their thirties to sixties cut their faces, arms and chests with sharp shells and shark teeth until the blood runs, as the haehae of the Māori and the old mourning of Hawaiʻi and Tahiti required
in Samoa mourners in their twenties to seventies in white clothes sit around the body on fine woven mats, a man in his fifties among them in a lavalava over the dense black pe'a tattoo that covers him from waist to knees
