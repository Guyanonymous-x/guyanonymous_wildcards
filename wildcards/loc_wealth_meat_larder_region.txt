the English north-wall larder as the type, universal in every house with servants from the sixteenth century to the 1930s
the Scottish version, cooler and therefore better, with a longer safe period
the Irish version, the same
the French garde-manger, identical in principle and often with a hanging cage outside the window
the Italian and Spanish versions, which rely on cellars and on salting and air-drying far more because the climate defeats a ventilated larder for five months of the year, which is why the charcuterie and jamón traditions exist and the English one does not
the Scandinavian and Alpine versions, which use a separate cold store or a snow-packed pit
a Russian estate ice house and cold cellar
the American spring house, which runs cold water over the shelves and is a materially better solution; and the Anglo-Indian household, where a ventilated larder is useless, meat is bought daily and killed that morning, and the real cold store is a wet-cloth safe in a draft
