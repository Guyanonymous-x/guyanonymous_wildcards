the English great kitchen as the type, with the detached or semi-detached plan as its single most characteristic feature
the medieval version, a separate stone building with a central hearth and a louvred roof, of which the Glastonbury and Durham examples are the survivors
the Scottish version, the same and colder
the French cuisine of a château, which is better equipped, closer to the dining room and produces hotter food, a difference English visitors remarked on repeatedly
the Italian and Spanish versions, with charcoal doing far more of the work and open fires less
the Russian estate kitchen, built round a masonry stove and a bread oven of enormous mass
the American plantation kitchen, a separate building for reasons of heat, fire and the fact that the people working in it were enslaved, which makes it a different institution in the same architectural form; and the Anglo-Indian kitchen, a detached godown in the compound with charcoal chulas on the floor and the cook squatting rather than standing, which changes every working height in the room
