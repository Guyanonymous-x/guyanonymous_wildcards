The morning check, with the module's seals examined, the drugs roll's seal number read against the card, oxygen contents noted, the mass and balance recalculated for the day's crew and fuel, and the cabin's loose-article check, which is done by hand on every surface
The standby, with the cabin doors open on the apron and nobody in it, which is where almost all the hours go
The launch, from pager to lift in four to six minutes with the crew boarding in sequence because only one person can enter at a time
The flight out, with the crew briefed on intercom and nothing to do
The scene, where the cabin is irrelevant and the work happens on the ground
The flight in, which is the cabin's whole purpose and its busiest fifteen to forty minutes, two people kneeling, a monitor swung over the chest, the sun in the wrong place and everything said twice over the intercom
The handover on a hospital pad, doors open, rotors running or shut down
The clean-down, done on the apron with the module hosed and the floor drained; and the stand-down at last light in those services that do not fly at night, which is a real operational boundary and changes what the aircraft is for
