the front room of a terraced house in 1972 with one monk, an image on a chest of drawers, a carpet, and no sign outside, which is where almost every one of these congregations in Britain began and is a type worth recording for itself
the converted suburban semi or detached house of 1985, two or three knocked-through rooms, the monks living upstairs and the hall downstairs, which is still the commonest arrangement in this country by a wide margin and carries all the oddities of a house doing a public job, a bay window behind the altar, a staircase in the corner, a kitchen built for a family cooking for sixty
the converted chapel or church hall, where the volume is right and nothing else is
the purpose-built temple of 2000 onward, with a proper hall, monks' quarters, a kitchen and sometimes a stupa in the garden, funded by a national community and frequently with craftsmen and materials from Thailand, Sri Lanka or Burma
the retreat center in a former country house, where the hall is a converted drawing room with a plaster ceiling rose above the altar
the lay meditation group meeting in a hired room with a folding altar in a flight case, which is a real and numerous type, the whole temple in two boxes, set up in ninety minutes and taken down after
the hall in decline, with an ageing congregation, one elderly monk; and the closed building, where the images and the relic are formally removed first and the carpet's worn patches are the last evidence of what it was, which is a detail of some force
