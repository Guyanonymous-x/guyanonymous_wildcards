the crypt of 1120 as built, a vaulted undercroft for a shrine or a relic with a stair down from the chancel and a processional route round it, which is the original function and survives legibly in a dozen English cathedrals
the crypt of 1700 as a commercial burial space, with vaults sold by faculty and a sexton's fee list, which is how a parish funded its fabric
the crypt of 1850 at its fullest and worst, overcrowded, the subject of public-health reports, and a direct cause of the legislation that closed it
the crypt of 1860 cleared, the vaults bricked up, the floor relaid and the space empty for the first time in six hundred years
the crypt of 1935 with the boiler in it
the crypt of 1975 with the boiler out, the vaults still sealed, and eleven feet of water in the sump corner because the drain gave up
the crypt of 2026 as a café, a meeting room, a lavatory block, a treasury or a shop, the commonest modern use by a wide margin, dry-lined, lit, heated, with a servery against the nine bricked-up vaults and a laminated history on the wall, which is a genuinely good reuse and pays for the roof above
the crypt under archaeological excavation, with a grid, a license, a Home Office authority for the remains and a report that becomes the parish's best record of itself
the crypt sealed and unentered, with the stair gated at the top and the key held by an archdeacon, which is a real and quite common condition; and the crypt open to the weather, where it is the last dry space under a ruined church and then the first flooded one, because a vault with no roof above it becomes a tank
