the brewhouse of 1700 brewing every ten days for a household of forty, with the beer allowance a stated condition of service
the same room in 1800 with a thermometer and then a saccharometer, which turns the process from judgement to measurement and is the single biggest technical change in this record
the room in 1860 brewing monthly, with commercial beer coming in by rail and the allowance commuted partly to money
the room in 1890 abandoned, the copper sold for scrap, the vats sawn in half for cattle troughs and the tun used as a rainwater butt, which is the commonest end and is why complete brewhouses are rare while brewhouse coppers' brick surrounds are everywhere
the room as a laundry, which is the single commonest reuse because the copper, the water, the floor and the drains are all already there
the room as a garage or a workshop
the room as a working brewery again in an estate run commercially, which happens
the room mothballed, drained, the oak kept wet in a bucket to stop the staves shrinking, which is the only way to mothball a wooden vessel and is usually not done
the room open to the weather, where the oak goes in ten years, the lead is stolen in one, and the brick copper surround with its fire door stands for two hundred
