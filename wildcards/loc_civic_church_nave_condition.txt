the nave of 1450 with no seating at all, a painted screen, a doom painting over the chancel arch and a floor of beaten earth, which is the original and is almost nowhere recoverable
the nave of 1600 after the Reformation, whitewashed over its paintings, the screen's loft cut away, the royal arms where the rood was and the first benches in
the nave of 1750 full of box pews with doors, the rents recorded in a book, a three-decker pulpit halfway down and a west gallery for the singers and the band, which is the arrangement the Victorians destroyed most thoroughly and which survives in perhaps a hundred churches
the nave of 1870 after a restoration, the box pews and the gallery swept away, uniform benches, encaustic tiles, an organ in a chamber, the walls scraped back to bare rubble because that was thought authentic, and the heating installed, which is the moment the fabric's decline begins
the nave of 1950 with a congregation of eighty
the nave of 2020 with a congregation of eleven, the pews removed at the west end for chairs and a coffee urn, a lavatory in the tower, a servery, a defibrillator by the door and a quinquennial inspection report listing six figures of work
the nave shared between a congregation and a community use, a post office counter, a food bank, a concert, which is the type's commonest survival route and is genuinely saving a number of them
the nave declared closed for worship and vested in a conservation trust, maintained, unheated and opened by a keyholder, which is the best end available
the nave converted to a dwelling, a climbing wall, a gallery or a flats scheme, which keeps the envelope and loses everything in this record; and the nave open to the weather, where a stone building with a lead roof survives the loss of the roof only until the lead is stolen, and then the nave fills with its own ceiling, self-seeded ash and buddleia in ten years, and the font and the ledger stones outlast everything
