the bridge of 1900, an open platform with a binnacle, a telegraph, a voice pipe and a canvas weather cloth, where the watchkeeper stood in the weather for four hours and the wheelhouse was a shelter rather than a room
the bridge of 1950, enclosed, with a gyro, three officers and a quartermaster on the wheel, which is four people doing what one now does
the bridge of 1975, with a collision-avoidance radar, satellite navigation and an unmanned engine room, which is the moment the manning falls
the bridge of 2010, with two ECDIS, an integrated bridge system, a 900-point alarm panel, a watch alarm and a crew of one, which is the arrangement described here and is the type at its most capable and its most isolated
the bridge in heavy weather, with the clear-view screen running, everything loose stowed, the chart table's drawers latched and nobody sitting down
the bridge in pilotage, which is its real moment, master, pilot, officer of the watch, a helmsman on the wheel and two wings manned
the bridge during a port-state control inspection, where the books, the charts, the correction log and the checklists are the subject and are where a ship is actually detained
the bridge of a laid-up vessel at anchor off a coast with a skeleton crew, the electronics bagged and dehumidifiers running, which is a common condition in a freight downturn
the bridge of an abandoned vessel with the crew unpaid and unable to leave
the bridge of a vessel at a breaker's beach, stripped of every instrument within a week by men with wrenches while the hull is cut up around it, which is where almost all of these rooms end, there is no weathered-ruin variant, because a ship either floats and is maintained or is dismantled
