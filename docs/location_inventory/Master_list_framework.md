# Location Inventory: 350 Spaces

## How this is organized

350 locations in 7 categories of 50. Each location lists **Items**, the loose objects commonly found there, and **Furniture & equipment**, the fixed or large pieces: furniture, appliances, fixtures and machinery.

## Describing a space: the full variable set

Objects are only one of about twelve dimensions. Two kitchens with identical inventories can be completely different spaces because of hour, ownership, wear and who is allowed in. The dimensions below are the ones that change a description of any space type, with the main values each can take.

### 1. Shell and geometry

Footprint and shape (square, galley, L-shaped, open-plan, irregular). Ceiling height and type (low, standard, vaulted, double-height, exposed structure). Number of levels, mezzanines, split levels. Structural features: columns, beams, load-bearing walls, alcoves, niches, bay windows. Floor level relative to grade (basement, ground, upper, rooftop).

### 2. Openings and circulation

Doors: how many, type (hinged, sliding, revolving, swinging service door, roll-up, hatch, trapdoor), which way they swing, whether they lock and from which side. Windows: number, size, operable or fixed, what they look onto, sightlines in and out. Entry and exit routes, including service entrances, fire exits, stairs, elevators, dumbwaiters, corridors. Dead ends versus through-routes. Whether the space is a destination or a passage.

### 3. Surfaces and materials

Floor (tile, concrete, hardwood, carpet, linoleum, gravel, grass), walls (drywall, brick, tile, panelling, glass, canvas), ceiling (plaster, acoustic tile, exposed ducts, beams, sky). Texture, hardness, whether surfaces are porous or wipeable. Material tells you about budget, era and maintenance, and it governs acoustics and smell retention.

### 4. Light

Natural light: direction, quantity, time-of-day pattern, skylights. Artificial light: type (fluorescent, incandescent, LED, candle, firelight, neon), colour temperature, brightness, whether it's even or pooled. Controls: switches, dimmers, timers, motion sensors. Dark corners and shadow patterns.

### 5. Sound

Ambient noise floor (machinery hum, HVAC, traffic, crowd, silence). Acoustics: reverberant, deadened, echoing. Sound sources: music, announcements, equipment, voices, water. Sound isolation — what can be heard from outside and what carries out. Many spaces are more recognizable by sound than by sight.

### 6. Smell, air and temperature

Dominant odours (food, chemicals, damp, livestock, perfume, fresh paint, exhaust). Air quality, ventilation and airflow. Temperature and humidity, and whether they're controlled or ambient. Draughts and still air.

### 7. Time and rhythm

Time of day and how the space changes across it. Day of week. Season. Operating hours versus closed hours — an empty restaurant at 3 a.m. is a different space from the same room at service. Cyclical rhythms: shift changes, rush periods, prep and closing routines, seasonal peaks.

### 8. Occupancy

Typical number of people present, and the range from empty to capacity. Roles present (staff, customers, residents, guests, performers, patients, inmates). Density and proximity. Movement pattern: static seated, milling, queued, processional, rushing. Who is allowed to be there and who is not.

### 9. Access, control and ownership

Public, semi-public, private or restricted. Who holds the key. Locks, badges, codes, ticketing, membership, reservations. Supervision: staffed, monitored by camera, unmonitored. Visibility from adjacent spaces. Whether entering uninvited is normal, awkward or prohibited.

### 10. Condition and age

New, well-maintained, worn, neglected, derelict. Cleanliness. Repairs, patches, improvisations. Period of construction and whether the fit-out matches it. Renovation layers — a Victorian building with 1970s wiring and 2020s signage.

### 11. Services and infrastructure

Water (supply, drains, taps, hoses). Gas. Electrical (outlets, voltage, panels, generators). Data and wi-fi. HVAC, extraction, refrigeration. Fire systems (alarms, sprinklers, extinguishers, suppression hoods). Waste handling. Lifts and hoists. Signage, wayfinding and regulatory notices.

### 12. Function and purpose

Primary function, secondary functions, and informal unsanctioned uses (the loading dock where people smoke, the stairwell used for private calls). Front-of-house versus back-of-house. Whether the space is designed for display or concealment, for lingering or for throughput.

### 13. Social and cultural register

Formality level. Class and price signals. Regional and national conventions. Dress expectations. Behavioural norms: quiet, raucous, reverent, transactional. Rituals associated with the space. Who feels at home there and who feels out of place.

### 14. Atmosphere and connotation

The accumulated impression: cosy, clinical, grand, oppressive, sterile, chaotic, hushed. This is an output of the dimensions above rather than an independent variable, but it's worth naming because it's what a reader or player actually remembers.

### 15. Relationship to adjacent spaces

What lies on the other side of each wall, above and below. What the space opens onto. Its position in a sequence (lobby → corridor → room). Buffer spaces and thresholds: vestibules, airlocks, porches, landings.

## Commonalities across all space types

A few patterns hold almost everywhere, and they're useful as a checklist:

- **Front and back.** Nearly every business has a public face and a hidden working zone, and the two have opposite rules about mess, noise and who may enter. Homes have it too (living room versus laundry).
- **Storage somewhere.** Every functioning space has an associated place where its supplies live, often adjacent and less attractive.
- **A threshold.** Spaces are marked at entry by a mat, a desk, a sign, a change of flooring or a change in light, signalling the shift in rules.
- **A control point.** Somewhere a person or device governs access: a host stand, a till, a reception desk, a turnstile, a gate.
- **Services concentrated.** Plumbing and electrical cluster, which is why bathrooms back onto kitchens and why utility rooms sit where they do.
- **Evidence of routine.** Worn paths, propped doors, handwritten notes, improvised fixes — the traces of how the space is actually used versus how it was designed.
- **An empty state and a full state.** Every space has both, and they can feel like different places.
- **A seasonal or daily cycle.** Even spaces that look static have one.

## Suggested record structure

For a consistent dataset, each location could carry these fields: name, category, indoor/outdoor, public/private, typical size range, typical ceiling type, primary function, secondary functions, typical occupancy range, access control, operating hours pattern, dominant materials, light character, sound character, dominant smells, items, furniture and equipment, adjacent spaces, condition variants, and atmosphere tags. Variant axes worth tagging separately: era, region, price tier, scale (independent versus institutional) and condition (pristine, working, derelict).
