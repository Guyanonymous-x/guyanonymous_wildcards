the English great house's one genuinely original room type, and a space built for a verb rather than a function, placed on the top floor of the Elizabethan and Jacobean house
with a continuous window range on the outer or garden side glazed in leaded lights between stone or timber mullions
usually a shallow barrel or a compartmented design with ribs and pendants, which is the room's chief decorative expense and is designed to be looked at while walking; two chimneypieces placed at the one-third points so that neither end of the room is more than a third of its length from a fire, which still leaves most of the room cold; window embrasures deep enough to seat two people and treated as a row of small rooms off the big one; a floor of oak board laid in long lengths with no carpet anywhere, because the room is for walking; doors at both ends and usually one in the middle; no bell pull at all in the early form, because this is not a room you are served in; and an orientation chosen so the window range faces the prospect, which means it frequently faces north or east and is therefore the coldest principal room in the house
