the tunnel of 1750 as a short vaulted link between a detached kitchen and a house, which is the modest and common version; which is the type at its fullest and is a direct product of a particular Victorian attitude to the visibility of work
the extreme version at Welbeck, where the Duke of Portland built miles of tunnels wide enough for carriages, which is the subject taken past any practical purpose and into something else entirely
the tunnel of 1900 with electric conduit, a telephone line and a hot-water main added, so it becomes primarily a service duct
the tunnel of 1950 abandoned, with the end doors bricked up and the sidewalk lights tarmacked over, which is overwhelmingly the commonest condition and is why so many of these exist unrecorded under lawns
the tunnel still in use in a house open to the public, carrying cables and occasionally visitors, which happens
the tunnel partly collapsed at a sidewalk light, which is always where it goes first
the tunnel in a demolished house, surviving complete beneath a housing estate or a field, which is common and is routinely rediscovered by a drainage contractor
