the room that absorbs the entire difference between the park and the principal floor, and the one service space whose existence is explained by mud, placed at the point where outdoor people enter, which is a back door
a garden door or the gun-room end of the service range, so that no boot ever crosses a principal floor and no coat is ever brushed in a bedroom; a floor of stone
quarry tile or brick
laid level and swept rather than washed
since the dirt here is dry as often as wet; a scraper fixed outside the door and a second inside it, which are the first two fittings in the sequence; a boot bench with a slatted rack under it so that thirty or forty pairs can stand in rows and drain; a stove with a flue, not for the comfort of the room but to dry boots and clothes, and the one piece of heating in the service establishment installed purely to dry things; a drying rail over the stove; a brushing frame standing clear of the walls so that a coat can be brushed from all sides without putting the dust back on it; a window
usually small and high, because this is a room of close work on dark objects and the light is poor, which is an honest contradiction and a real complaint; a door to the service corridor and a door or a short passage to the outside; and a bell pull that rings in the servants' hall
