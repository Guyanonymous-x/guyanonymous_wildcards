the chamber of 1650 with five bells rung from the ground floor of the tower with no chamber at all, which is the earlier arrangement and survives in small towers
the chamber of 1821 with the first peal board, which is where most of these rooms' records begin
the chamber of 1890 with eight bells rehung on a cast-iron frame by one of the great founders, which is the type at its height and is when most of the boards were put up
the chamber of 1957 with the band photograph on the wall and ten ringers
the chamber of 2026 with four ringers, two of them over eighty, a simulator so that one person can practice alone without disturbing the village, and the bells rung for ten minutes before a service rather than for a quarter peal, which is the honest present condition of the great majority of these rooms
the chamber of a tower where the bells have been declared unringable on a structural report, with the ropes tied up permanently and the frame listed as being at risk, which is a common and quietly final condition
the chamber used as a store because the band folded, with the ropes coiled on a hook and the boards still on the wall, which is the saddest version and is numerous
the chamber in a redundant church vested in a trust, the bells maintained and rung twice a year by a visiting band; and the chamber open to the weather, where the ropes rot in two winters, the boards delaminate, the oak frame survives for decades and the bells survive indefinitely, so that a ruined tower with a complete ring of bells in it is a real and recurring find
