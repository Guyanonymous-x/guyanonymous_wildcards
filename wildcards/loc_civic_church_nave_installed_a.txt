the building type with the longest continuous use of anything in this document, and one whose plan is a thousand-year argument about who stands where, a nave of four to seven bays with an arcade on one or both sides carrying an aisle
a timber roof of tie-beam
a chancel arch at the east end and a tower arch at the west, which together produce the type's defining division - the nave is the parish's and the chancel is the priest's
since the parishioners were responsible for repairing the nave and the rector for the chancel, and that split survives in the fabric as two different qualities of masonry and two different dates of roof in a great many churches; a rood screen across the chancel arch in the medieval arrangement
mutilated or rebuilt at the Reformation and then variously restored, so that what stands there now dates the building's theology more precisely than anything else in it; seating that is the plan's social record, open benches in the fifteenth century
the rents abolished and the pews cut down or replaced with uniform benches in the nineteenth, and chairs replacing pews in the twentieth to make the nave usable for anything other than a service, which is the live argument in most parishes now; a font at the west end near the door, because baptism is entry; a pulpit on the north side at the nave's mid-point
glazed in clear quarries or in Victorian stained glass; a floor of ledger stones
encaustic tile or boards over a void; heating that in almost every case was installed in the nineteenth century
since nothing may be altered
