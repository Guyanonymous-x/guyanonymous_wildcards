a method developed in the sand hills of Nebraska in the 1890s when baling machines arrived before timber did
revived from the 1980s and now built in thousands worldwide, walls of straw bales laid flat or on edge
strawbale walls within a timber frame
compressed with straps
rendered both sides in lime or clay plaster which is the structural skin and the fire protection and must be vapor-permeable
a wide roof overhang and a raised plinth because water is the only real enemy
exceptional insulation of the order of three to four times a conventional wall
surprising fire resistance once rendered because dense straw has no oxygen in it
a build process that lends itself to volunteers and wall-raising parties, and a building control and insurance conversation that every owner has had to have
